Why it stops where it stops

Water pushes up on anything in it. That push, the buoyant force, equals the weight of the water the object shoves aside. A floating object settles lower and lower until it has pushed aside its own weight in water, then it stops. An orange with its peel on is about 0.87 times as dense as water, so it rides with about 87% of itself underwater.

If an object goes all the way under and still hasn't pushed aside its own weight, nothing more can hold it up, and it sinks to the bottom.

Why salt helps

Salt makes water denser, so every cupful pushed aside weighs more and pushes up harder. Stir in enough and the potato floats. Ships feel the same thing: a cargo ship rides higher in the ocean than in a river, and its hull carries a painted load line with separate marks for fresh water and sea water.

Run it in your kitchen

Everything here costs a few dollars at the grocery store. Get a friend to call float or sink before each drop.

You need

  • A clear bin or a big bowl
  • Water, about two-thirds full
  • A potato, a cantaloupe, grapes, an orange, a grapefruit
  • Salt and a tall glass, for the bonus round

Steps

  1. Fill the bin with room-temperature water.
  2. Hold each item above the water and have everyone call it.
  3. Lower it in gently. Look at how much of it sits under the waterline.
  4. Peel the orange and try it again. Then peel the grapefruit.
  5. Put a grape in the glass of water and stir in salt one spoonful at a time. Count the spoonfuls until it rises.

Then try

  • An appleFloats. About a quarter of an apple is air.
  • A lemon and a limeThe lemon usually floats and the lime often sinks. Guess which before you look it up.
  • An eggSinks in tap water. Keep adding salt and it comes up.
